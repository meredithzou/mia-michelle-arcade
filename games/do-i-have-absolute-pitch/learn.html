Before getting started

This is our family’s interpretation of someone else’s published research, shared for information and informal experimentation. It is not an independently validated training program, professional instruction, or a promise of developing absolute pitch.

Users are encouraged to read the original source and decide whether the research and these activities are suitable or useful for them.

01 · THE RESEARCH

What we read

PRIMARY SOURCE · OPEN ACCESS

Learning fast and accurate absolute pitch judgment in adulthood ↗

Yetta Kwailing Wong, Leo Y. T. Cheung, Vince S. H. Ngan & Alan C.-N. Wong (2025). Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 32, 1676–1688.

What the paper did. The analysis included 12 experienced adult musicians who trained over eight weeks, averaging 21.4 hours. The program helped participants learn to name notes by ear across three octaves, starting with F and gradually adding more note names.

What the researchers found. By the end of training, participants could name an average of 7.08 note names at 90% accuracy or above, without feedback and within the required time. In a separate piano test covering all 12 note names, average accuracy increased from 13.9% before training to 31.7% afterwards.

02 · THE PAPER’S CORE METHOD

How the training worked

The researchers started with one note name, F. Participants learned to recognize it among nearby notes, then gradually learned more names. Sounds came from three octaves, C4–B6, throughout training.

  1. Measure the starting point. Before training, participants heard notes and chose their names from all 12 possibilities. No answers were revealed. The researchers measured accuracy, how far wrong answers were from the actual note, and response time.
  2. Learn to recognize F. Participants heard F4, F5 or F6 mixed with E♭, E, F♯ and G in the same three octaves. The answer was “F” for any F, or “out of bound” for the other notes.
  3. Become more accurate and faster. Each set of note names had 24 training levels. Early levels gave correct/incorrect feedback. Later levels removed feedback and allowed less answer time. The final level required at least 90% accuracy within its time limit.
  4. Add a name and keep the earlier ones. After completing those levels for F, participants learned E as well: answers became E, F or “out of bound.” Next came F♯: E, F, F♯ or “out of bound.” This continued toward all 12 names.
  5. Test again after training. Participants took another separate test covering all 12 names, without feedback, so the researchers could compare performance before and after training.
WHAT TARGET AND NON-TARGET MEAN

Target: a note name in the current learning set. Each target has its own answer. Non-target: one of the other notes mixed into the practice. The paper calls this answer “out of bound”; our practice calls it “Non-target.”

Source: the paper’s Training and Pre-post tests sections.

About the sound

This family experiment uses recorded piano samples to generate notes. Sound accuracy and training results are not guaranteed; users decide whether the tool is suitable and whether to rely on it.

Sound source, processing and limitations

The sound source is Salamander Grand Piano V3 by Alexander Holm (CC BY 3.0), MP3 velocity layer 8, collection v1.0.5.

The tool changes a nearby sample’s playback rate to generate each note, using A4 = 440 Hz and equal-temperament ratios, and applies a volume envelope. It does not run the full SFZ instrument or its tuning adjustments.

We have not independently verified every rendered note’s frequency. This is best-effort playback, not a calibrated pitch reference or a validated training program.
